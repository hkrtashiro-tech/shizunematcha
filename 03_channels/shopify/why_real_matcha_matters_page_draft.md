### 2. The matcha boom has a shadow（ブームの影）

> Matcha's popularity has grown faster than Japan can grow the leaf. True matcha begins with *tencha* — shade-grown leaves, slowly stone-milled — and there is only so much of it each harvest. When demand far outpaces supply, that gap is where shortcuts appear.
>
> Most of what's sold as "matcha" today is bright, green, and photogenic. But color alone tells you very little. The real story is in the leaf, the origin, and the maker — and those are exactly the things a label can hide.

---

### 3. Where the label stops telling the truth（ラベルが嘘をつき始めるところ）

> The matcha aisle is full of confident words: *ceremonial*, *Uji*, *stone-ground*. Each one sounds like a guarantee. Too often, it isn't. Here are three of the most common — and what they actually mean once you look closely.

**1. "Ceremonial grade" is not the grade you think it is**
> Outside Japan, "ceremonial grade" has been flattened into a single marketing phrase — used, at best, to mean "for drinking rather than baking." That's it. It tells you the powder isn't meant for cookies. It tells you almost nothing about quality.
>
> Japan doesn't work that way. Real matcha is judged on a strict, layered scale — leaf age, harvest timing, milling, finish. Two matcha can both be sold abroad as *"ceremonial,"* yet sit worlds apart: one a basic latte powder, the other a fine *usucha* made to be whisked and sipped on its own. Same word on the tin. Completely different tea. When one term is stretched to cover everything, it stops protecting the buyer — and starts protecting the seller.

**2. The "Uji" math doesn't add up**
> *Uji* is one of the most prestigious names in matcha — and one of the most borrowed. Here's the uncomfortable part: the amount of authentic Uji-region matcha Japan actually produces each year is a small, finite figure. The amount of matcha sold around the world labeled *"Uji"* is dramatically larger.
>
> Those two numbers cannot both be true. A respected region has quietly become a marketing word, printed on tins filled with powder grown somewhere else. If a famous origin is on the label, the real question isn't *"is it Uji?"* — it's *"can they prove it?"*

**3. "Stone-ground" — possible to print, hard to mean**
> True stone milling is slow. A single granite mill produces only a tiny amount of matcha per hour, which is exactly why genuinely stone-ground matcha is expensive to make. So when a low-priced matcha proudly says *"stone-ground"* on the front, the math gets suspicious fast.
>
> Either the volume and the price don't match the method — or the word is being used loosely for something else. "Stone-ground" should signal care and cost. On a bargain tin, it more often signals a story that doesn't hold up.

### 7. ミニFAQ（よくある質問）

**Is some matcha fake?**
> "Fake" is strong — but yes, some matcha is diluted with cheaper green tea or fillers, and some is mislabeled by origin. It's rarely obvious from a photo, which is why traceability matters more than color.

**Does a famous region like "Uji" on the label guarantee quality?**
> No. Far more matcha is sold worldwide as "Uji" than the region could possibly produce — the numbers simply don't add up. A region name only means something when the brand can trace the leaf back to it. Ask where it's grown and who makes it; an honest maker will answer.

**What does "ceremonial grade" really mean?**
> Outside Japan, it's mostly used to mean "for drinking, not baking" — and nothing more. In Japan, drinking matcha spans a strict, layered scale, so two "ceremonial" matcha can be completely different teas. We grade honestly, by what's actually in the bowl — every SHIZUNE matcha is made to be whisked and sipped on its own.

**Isn't all matcha "stone-ground"?**
> It shouldn't be assumed. Real stone milling is slow and low-volume, which makes it genuinely expensive. When a cheap matcha claims it, the price and the method rarely match up — so the claim is worth questioning.

**How do I know SHIZUNE is different?**
> Because we name our origin (currently Shizuoka), keep our matcha 100% pure, and make it in Japan by Japanese makers. Traceable by origin, and never mixed.
