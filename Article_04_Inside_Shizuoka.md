# Inside Shizuoka: The Region Behind Our Matcha

When people think of Japanese tea, Kyoto often comes to mind first — and for good reason. Uji, in Kyoto Prefecture, has centuries of history as the birthplace of matcha culture. But Japan's tea landscape is far richer and more diverse than any single region.

Shizuoka Prefecture, located along Japan's Pacific coast between Tokyo and Nagoya, is the country's largest tea-producing region. It accounts for roughly 40% of all tea grown in Japan. And while it's long been known for sencha, Shizuoka's matcha has been quietly gaining recognition for its distinct character — clean, balanced, and deeply connected to the land.

This is where Shizune Matcha begins.

---

## Where Shizuoka Sits — and Why It Matters

Shizuoka stretches along the southern coast of Honshu, Japan's main island. To the north, the peaks of the Japanese Southern Alps rise sharply. To the south, the warm Kuroshio Current flows along the Pacific coast. And to the west, on clear mornings, Mount Fuji watches over the tea fields in silence.

This geography creates a unique growing environment. The combination of mineral-rich volcanic soil, clean mountain water, coastal humidity, and significant temperature shifts between day and night gives Shizuoka tea its character — a clean, bright flavor with a natural sweetness that's distinct from teas grown in other regions.

Tea has been cultivated here for over 800 years. What began as small hillside gardens has grown into a region-wide tradition, passed down through generations of farming families who understand the rhythms of the land.

## The Shizuoka Approach to Tea

Every tea region in Japan has its own philosophy. In Shizuoka, the approach tends to be quiet and practical — focused on consistency, care, and respect for the natural environment.

### Shade-Growing for Matcha

Like all high-quality matcha, the tea leaves used for Shizune Matcha are shade-grown. About three to four weeks before harvest, the fields are covered to block most sunlight. This encourages the plants to produce more chlorophyll and L-theanine, resulting in a deeper green color, richer umami, and a smoother, less bitter taste.

In Shizuoka, many farmers use traditional shelf-style shading structures called *tana*, which allow careful control of light levels while maintaining airflow around the plants. This attention to detail during the shading period is one of the factors that shapes the final flavor of the matcha.

### Harvest and Selection

The first harvest of the year — known as *ichibancha* (一番茶) — takes place in spring, typically from late April to May. These first-flush leaves are the most prized, carrying the highest concentration of nutrients and the most refined flavor.

Only the youngest, most tender leaves at the top of the plant are selected for matcha production. After picking, the leaves are quickly steamed to stop oxidation, then dried. At this stage, the processed leaves are called *tencha* (碾茶) — the raw material of matcha.

### Stone Grinding

The final step is grinding the tencha into a fine powder using traditional granite stone mills. This process is slow by design — each mill produces only about 30 to 40 grams of matcha per hour. The low speed prevents heat buildup, preserving the delicate aroma, vibrant color, and nutritional integrity of the leaves.

It's a patient, unhurried process — and that patience is something you can taste.

## Shizuoka and Uji: Two Traditions, One Craft

When discussing Japanese matcha, the comparison between Shizuoka and Uji (Kyoto) comes up naturally. Uji is the historical heart of matcha — the place where tea ceremony culture was born and refined over centuries. Its matcha is renowned for deep umami complexity and a rich, full-bodied character.

Shizuoka's matcha tradition is younger, but it draws on something different: the region's long, deep expertise in cultivating tea at scale, combined with its exceptional natural environment. Where Uji matcha tends toward depth and intensity, Shizuoka matcha leans toward clarity and balance.

Neither is "better" — they reflect different landscapes, climates, and philosophies. What we love about Shizuoka is how its character speaks quietly. It doesn't demand attention. It simply offers something honest and satisfying each time you drink it.

## What Makes Shizuoka Matcha Different?

Every tea region produces matcha with its own personality. Here's how Shizuoka's character tends to stand out:

**Clean and balanced.** Shizuoka matcha is often described as having a clear, straightforward flavor — rich in umami but without heaviness. It's smooth, approachable, and versatile.

**Naturally sweet.** The combination of volcanic soil, clean water, and careful shading tends to produce a gentle natural sweetness that lingers after each sip.

**Bright green color.** The high chlorophyll content from shade-growing gives Shizuoka matcha a vivid, lively green — a visual indicator of freshness and quality.

**Versatile.** Whether you're whisking a traditional [usucha](/blogs/recipes/usucha), making a [latte](/blogs/recipes/hot-matcha-latte), or blending a [smoothie bowl](/blogs/recipes/matcha-smoothie-bowl), Shizuoka matcha adapts beautifully to different preparations.

## Tea Culture in Shizuoka

In Shizuoka, tea is not a luxury or a trend. It's a part of daily life.

Families drink tea with meals. Neighbors share tea during visits. Farmers taste their harvest to understand what the season has given them. Children grow up surrounded by tea fields — the landscape itself is a constant, green reminder of what this region has always been.

There's no ceremony required — just the simple, repeated act of making tea, pouring it, and sharing a moment of warmth. In many Shizuoka homes, the kettle is always on, and tea is offered before words.

This everyday relationship with tea is something we carry into Shizune Matcha. We believe matcha doesn't need to be reserved for special occasions or elaborate rituals. It belongs in your morning, your afternoon break, your quiet moment before the day begins.
